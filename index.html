zip-password-finder

Native Rust. Multi-threaded. One self-contained binary.

  • ZIP + 7z both, auto-detected
  • 3 attacks brute force, dictionary, mask
  • All cores one worker per physical core
  • 1 binary no runtime deps

ZIP and 7z alike

ZipCrypto and AES ZIPs, and AES-256 7z archives including header-encrypted ones. The type is read from the file's contents, not its name, and the encrypted entry is found for you.

Three ways in

Brute force a combined charset, feed a wordlist, or write a mask like ?u?l?l?l?d?d when you remember the shape. Custom charsets and resuming from a known point are built in.

Made to go fast

Every physical core runs a worker. ZIP uses a cheap per-candidate verifier; a native build with SHA instructions goes faster still. Ctrl-C stops cleanly and shows the last password tried.

Pick the attack that fits

The same three modes work on both ZIP and 7z. Give a mode, or let it brute force by default.

Brute force

Every password in a charset, across a length range. The default when no wordlist or mask is given.

$ zip-password-finder archive.zip -c lud --max-password-len 6

Dictionary

Test the words of a file, one per line — the realistic choice for slow 7z archives.

$ zip-password-finder archive.7z -p wordlist.txt

Mask

A per-position pattern for a password whose shape you remember: one uppercase, three lowercase, two digits.

$ zip-password-finder archive.zip --mask '?u?l?l?l?d?d'

Tuned for throughput

By default it runs one worker per physical core — more than that tends to lose to contention. For AES, a CPU with SHA instructions and a target-cpu=native build make the biggest difference.

A word on 7z

7z derives its AES-256 key by iterating SHA-256 about 524,288 times per candidate, against 1,000 rounds for ZIP-AES, so throughput is far lower and brute force is impractical beyond very short passwords. Reach for a dictionary or mask attack there. AES-256 only, which is what every recent 7z build writes.

Tips & common questions

How long will it take?

Brute force grows exponentially — each extra character, or a wider charset, can turn minutes into years. If you remember any of the password's shape, a mask attack shrinks the space dramatically; otherwise a wordlist is usually faster than trying every combination. The progress bar shows a live ETA so you can decide whether to narrow the search.

It printed "Password not found" — now what?

That means the password simply was not in the space you searched, not that the archive is unbreakable. Widen the charset (-c), raise --max-password-len, or switch to a wordlist. Exit codes follow grep: 0 found, 1 searched but not found, 2 an error.

Can I stop and resume a long run?

Yes. Ctrl-C stops cleanly and prints the last password it tried. For a brute-force search, start again from there with --starting-password so you do not retread ground.

How do I make it faster?

Build native with RUSTFLAGS="-C target-cpu=native", and for AES use a CPU with SHA instructions. Keep the worker count at your physical cores (the default) — more threads mostly add contention. Above all, narrow the search: a mask or a wordlist beats raw brute force every time.

Does it handle AES and 7z archives?

Yes — ZipCrypto and AES ZIPs, and AES-256 7z including header-encrypted ones (-mhe=on). The format is detected from the file's contents, not its extension, and all three attack modes work the same on both. Expect 7z to be much slower, so favour a dictionary or mask there.

Is it legal to use?

It is a recovery tool, for archives you own or are explicitly authorized to access — a forgotten password on your own backup, say. Using it against files that are not yours is on you.
